What do these fortunes cost the rest of us?

There is no single bill. A fortune can cost the public through some channels and give back through others. For each channel, here is what the evidence says and the strongest objection to it.

Tax

Billionaire wealth grew by about 7.5% a year above inflation over four decades, while the taxes billionaires pay amount to about 0.3% of their wealth each year. A minimum tax of 2% of wealth on about 3,000 billionaires would raise $200 billion to $250 billion a year, according to a report to the G20 by Gabriel Zucman.

Objection. Most of this wealth is unsold company shares, which are hard to value and whose profits are already taxed at company level. Critics of wealth taxes also point to liquidity problems and capital flight.

Zucman, report to the G20, 2024

Public money

Large fortunes are often built with public support. Elon Musk’s companies alone received at least $38 billion in public contracts, loans, subsidies and tax credits over two decades, according to the Washington Post.

Objection. Much of this money pays for services the state chose to buy, and supporters argue that SpaceX lowered launch costs for the public.

Washington Post, 2025

Climate

Oxfam estimated in 2022 that the investments of 125 of the richest billionaires emit about 3 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent each per year, roughly a million times the 2.76 tonnes of a person in the poorest 90% of humanity.

Objection. Assigning a company’s emissions to its owners is a choice; the same emissions can be assigned to the customers who buy its products. Owners can also steer companies towards lower emissions.

Oxfam, Carbon Billionaires, 2022

Political power

In the 2024 US election cycle, six donors each gave more than $100 million; before 2018 no individual had given that much in a cycle. In the 2026 midterms, Elon Musk alone gave about $445 million.

Objection. Spending does not guarantee results, large donors exist on both sides, and US courts treat political spending as protected speech.

Election Law Blog, from OpenSecrets data; Newsweek, 2026

Innovation and value

William Nordhaus estimated that innovators keep only about 2.2% of the social value of their innovations; the rest reaches consumers as lower prices and better products. Steven Kaplan and Joshua Rauh argue that top fortunes mostly reflect market returns to talent and scale.

Objection. Josh Bivens and Lawrence Mishel argue that much top income is rent, captured through market power and finance rather than created.

Nordhaus, 2004; Kaplan and Rauh, 2013; Bivens and Mishel, 2013; Mankiw, 2013

Inheritance

Of these hundred fortunes, 62 were built by their current holder and 26 were inherited from a parent or a spouse. Across all billionaires, UBS counted a record $297.8 billion inherited by 91 heirs in 2025 and expects $5.9 trillion to pass to children within 15 years.

Objection. The same UBS report counted 196 new self-made billionaires in 2025, the most since 2021.

UBS, Billionaire Ambitions Report, 2025

Is it acceptable to have fortunes this large?

The evidence above can show what a fortune costs and contributes through each channel. It cannot decide how to weigh them, because that depends on values. Three positions in political philosophy frame the question. John Rawls argued that inequalities are just only if they benefit the least advantaged. Robert Nozick argued that holdings are just if they were acquired and transferred justly, whatever distribution results. Ingrid Robeyns argues for an upper limit on personal wealth, because extreme fortunes turn into political power and are not needed for anyone to flourish.

This site does not choose between them. It tries to make the facts each position needs easy to check.

What would a levy raise?

Apply a hypothetical yearly levy to the combined net worth of the hundred. This compares orders of magnitude only. Real receipts would depend on valuation, avoidance, enforcement, jurisdiction and market effects.

At 2% a year, the levy would raise about $142B.

The marker shows $310B, the lower estimate of yearly adaptation needs in developing countries.

The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) estimates that developing countries will need $310B to $365B a year for climate adaptation by 2035. International public finance for adaptation reached $26B in 2023. UNEP Adaptation Gap Report 2025

What is measured

Names, ranks and net worths come from the Forbes annual list of 1 March 2026. Tied entries share a rank. A net worth is an estimate of asset values. It is not cash, it moves with markets, and this list is not updated between editions.

How the scores work

Each fortune gets five scores from 0 to 4. A score is documented when a linked source supports it, and an estimate when it comes from the sector rubric alone. Estimates are hatched everywhere on the site. A company’s settlement is not a personal conviction, and allegations are described as allegations. Open each scale to see what every level means.

Totals and colours

The total is a plain sum from 0 to 20, used for sorting and colour. It is crude: an old tax penalty and a mine disaster can add up to the same number, so read the profile. Only profiles with at least one documented score get a colour: 0 to 4 is lower concern, 5 to 8 mixed, 9 or more high concern. The others stay grey until someone checks them, because an unchecked fortune scoring low would only mean nobody has looked.

About the Epstein files

In January 2026 the US Justice Department released about 3.5 million of more than 6 million identified pages, many of them redacted, and said the release was complete. Lawmakers have questioned what was withheld. Being named in the files is not evidence of a crime. Where released documents raise unanswered questions about a person, this site scores them under open questions, records the person’s denial, and does not treat withheld pages as proof of anything.

What remains unknown

Full portfolio holdings, the attribution of corporate emissions, tax exposure, political networks and positive effects all need deeper review. The absence of a known controversy does not prove a clean record, and a high score does not establish criminal behaviour.
